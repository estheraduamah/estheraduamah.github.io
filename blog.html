Designing Assistive AI Without Eroding Human Agency

By Esther Aduamah · Boston, MA · Notes from the Human-Centered Computing Lab

When technologists design tools for older adults, there is a quiet temptation to equate helpfulness with full automation—to strip away decisions until a button simply does the task on the user’s behalf. Yet across months of co-design workshops around digital patient portals, participants repeatedly pushed back against tools that tried to think for them.

One participant phrased the tension with unforgettable clarity: “Should I use this or my mind?” That single question reframed how our lab thinks about multimodal wearable assistants. Good scaffolding lowers administrative friction—translating clinical jargon or orienting attention—while leaving judgment, memory, and agency firmly in the user’s hands.

Chimera Ant

By Esther Aduamah · August 10, 2024 · Speculative Fiction & Memory

For once in my prepubescent life, I wished I had put on some deodorant. My sweat is painting some Picasso designs on my underarms. I don’t know who would put children in a smothering hot car on the equator, but somehow, we were sitting in there. Even the ants on the car floor are dying from the heat.

“When is Rosemary and Aunt Sister be back?” Junior asks (yes, that’s her real name; life has a way of working that way when you’re the youngest sister, and your parents stopped being creative with names after child five).

“I think in a half hour or so. Rosemary broke some needles so she’s gotta get some new needles,” I replied. Raph nods.

Featured Profile · Science Communication

Dr. Kim McCall: Unraveling Programmed Cell Death in Drosophila

By Esther Aduamah · February 20, 2025 · Developmental Biology & Neurobiology

Imagine dedicating 25 years to unraveling the mysteries of developmental biology, and still going strong even when you’re not feeling your best. That’s Professor Dr. Kim McCall for you, a biology professor (who used to be the chair of biology!) at Boston University who has spent over two decades advancing our understanding of programmed cell death (PCD) and cell clearance. When I had the chance to talk to her, she was under the weather, but her passion and dedication shone through, reminding us that even the most accomplished scientists are, after all, human.

Her interest in Drosophila (fruit flies) actually started way back in high school during an AP biology class. That spark stayed with her through college, where she got involved in a Drosophila genetics lab. Later, she joined the Jan lab at UCSF, where she dove deeper into fly genetics and the study of the nervous system. It was there that she realized how powerful Drosophila could be for studying neurobiology, something that’s remained a focus throughout her career.
