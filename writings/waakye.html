Special Equipment

Dadesɛn Pot

Ingredients

  • 2 cups Local brown rice or parboiled rice
  • Palm Nut Oil
  • Shea Butter Oil
  • 2 cups Dried black-eyed peas
  • 10-15 Dried sorghum leaves
  • 1 tsp Kanwe (Potash)
  • Salt to taste
  • Water

“You see that poor girl’s neck.” I glance up at the hot sun almost blinding my view of her. She’s carrying two pans on her filled with food to sell. Her face is so young, Kayayo, she must be seventeen, maybe sixteen. Everyone buys clothes from Foss, but the way their bodies disappear under all of them is obvious that she’s eating too little and working too hard.

“Hey! Bra ha.”

I wave out for her to come. She walks over, adjusting the aluminum pan on head, making sure she remains balanced.

“I need someone to help me move rice bags later,” I said, which is true but not the whole truth.

“What's your name?”

“Ayishetu.”

I saw it in her face, but it did confirm she’s from the north.

“Ma’am, how should I refer to you?”

“My name is Afia, call me Mrs. Afia.”

“Yes, Mrs. Afia.”

“How old are you, girl?”

“Eighteen.”

“You're lying.”

The girl looks at me for a long moment. Then she shrugs, carefully, so the yams don't shift.

“Sixteen. But I'll be seventeen soon.”

“Here come help me. You can still get yourself some food even when you’re under eighteen.”

“No thank you, ma’am I have my own food.”

Probably some biscuits in her pocket.

“Hmm you sure, don’t you want something hot to eat.”

These girls are always in Makola Market, starving. It’s such a shame. And they’ve scared of everyone in the market and outside the market. I turn back to look at her, and her scrawny ass should definitely eat.

“Help me carry the rice on tro tros to my place once we’re there. We can cook you something to eat.”

She seemed a bit nervous, but I knew and she knew that if she stayed here while it got too dark it’d be dangerous. She’d be hungry and weak, and an easy target for people in the streets.

So she came.

We sat on the tro tros, holding the rice tightly to our chest.

We got to my family home and I sat her by the coalpot outside.

“Okay, if we’re going to cook, we have to cook for the whole house. Now what would you like to make?”

“Let’s make waakye!”

What the hell is waakye? She somehow already knows what I’m thinking because before I can ask she goes,

“It’s a northern dish, it’s really good you know. It’s rice and beans.”

I chuckle,

“I think we can manage that”

She stands up, suddenly less tired.

“Do you have sorghum leaves? Dried ones?”

“Sorghum leaves?”

“For the color. That's what makes it waakye. Otherwise it's just rice and beans.”

I found some dried sorghum leaves in the back of the pantry, left from my mother-in-law's last visit. She's northern too, from Tamale, and she left them here like a test I didn't know I was taking. When I hand them to Ayishetu, her whole face changes.

"These are good ones," she grins, turning them over in her hands. "My grandmother used to say the darker the leaf, the better the waakye."

"Your grandmother taught you?"

"She taught me everything. Before she died."

She says it like she’s talking about the weather.

"She said waakye is just starting to walk from the north down to the south, and now everyone eats it and forgets where it came from."

I watch her rinse the leaves. Her hands move like how my grandmother’s used to: quick, sure, no wasted motion. She's so young but her hands are already so old.

"You know," I say, "my mother-in-law is northern. Muslim. When I married her son, she looked at me like I was stealing something. A Ga woman, Christian, taking her boy."

I start sorting the beans, picking out stones.

Ayishetu looks up from the leaves. "My boyfriend's mother is the same. She says I'm too young, that I'm just a Kayayo, that I'll never be enough for her son."

She shrugs, but it's not casual.

"I'm going back next month. My mother is sick. When I go back, we'll marry. She'll have to accept me then."

"How old is this boyfriend?"

"Twenty."

"And he's good to you?"

She hesitates.

"He doesn't hit me. He works. When I'm tired, he doesn't complain that dinner is late."

She says it like she's listing qualifications for a job. Maybe she is.

"That's good," I don’t push further.

The beans are boiling now. Ayishetu adds some of the kanwe and the water changes color. She stirs, then steps back, letting me add the rice. We work without talking for a while. The evening is coming in, the sun is dropping behind the compound walls, and somewhere a neighbor is playing highlife on a radio.

"When you go back," I say, "are you scared?"

"Of what?"

"Of being a wife. Of being in the north. Of—"

I don't know how to say it. But I feel in my heart that something is not right with her situation.

"I've been scared since I came here," she says, not looking at me. "At least at home I'll be scared with people who know my name and my food."

The waakye finishes cooking. We plate it together, and she adds the gari on the side the way her grandmother showed her, and I fry eggs for the children, and for a little while the kitchen just smells like food.

"Someday," I tell her as she eats, "when you have your own kitchen, you teach your daughter this. You tell her it came from the north. You tell her it's hers."

Ayishetu nods, mouth full. "I will, Mrs. Afia."

She left the next morning, early, before the market woke up. I gave her some of the leftover waakye wrapped in newspaper and two of my old wrappers I don't wear anymore. She tried to refuse, but I told her to shut up and take them.

I haven't seen her since. But sometimes I think about her in the north, in some man’s kitchen, making waakye with sorghum leaves and kanwe, and I hope she tells her daughter about the woman in Makola who taught her that it was hers all along.

Shit, I still haven’t turned the eggs on. I turn around to turn the eggs to boil with some salt and then again.

“Hey you know what Mark, I think I got it.”

“Hm?”

“It's funny, I saw that bread pudding in your fridge yesterday that you made for yourself, so I know you know how to use a kitchen. But you can't peel an onion?”

He looks down for a bit and then bounces back up, “Ah okay, you know I’ll do what I do best.”

I raise an eyebrow.

“Drinking wine of course.”

I take my mom’s shito out of the fridge because I’m not even going to attempt that.

“You want wine?”

“No thanks.”

He leans against the counter while I'm running between the goat and the stew and the eggs.

“So what were you saying about the Master's programs?”

I wait for him to offer to help again. He doesn't. Just sips his wine.

“I got into BU and Michigan.”

“Both? That's great.”

“Michigan's offering more money.”

“So go to Michigan.”

“Mark, it’s like a twelve-hour drive.”

I should obviously go to Michigan. Yes, I’m still figuring it out, but if that’s the best choice, that’s what I’m doing. I just want him to say something, be a little sad or something. Tell me to go and that he’ll be fighting or something.

“People do long distance.”

I stir the stew.

“People also break up doing long distance.”

“Amber, are you really worried about that?"

“Aren’t you?”

He shrugs.

“I mean, we'll figure it out. You're smart. I'm—”

He gestures vaguely.

"I'm here."

“Could you set the table?”

He looks a little confused, but still goes,

“Yeah, I’m on it.”

The thing is, Mark is nice. He really is. He shows up on time, he brings wine, he tells me I'm smart and beautiful and all the things men are supposed to say. But I'm in the kitchen doing everything and he can't peel an onion and when I try to talk about the future he says we'll figure it out like the future is a surprise party and not something you have to build with your hands.

He puts his phone down and starts grabbing plates from the wrong cabinet. I don't correct him. If the plates don't match, they don't match. I'm too tired.

We eat. The waakye is good. The stew is a little burnt but whatever. Mark says “this is great, babe” with his mouth full and I say thanks and we don't talk about Michigan, Boston, or anything academic again.

After dinner, I get up to do the dishes because I hate going to bed with a dirty kitchen and he’d rather relax before cleaning up. I eventually joined him on the couch and wondered when having to ask became the whole relationship.
